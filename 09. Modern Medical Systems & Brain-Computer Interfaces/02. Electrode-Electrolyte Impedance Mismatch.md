**❌ Core Physical Deadlock:** The electrode-electrolyte interface is not a resistor that can be reduced by "better materials." It is a double-layer capacitor in series with a Faradaic charge-transfer resistance. The interfacial impedance magnitude is bounded by:

|Z| = sqrt(R_ct² + (1/ωC_dl)²)

where C_dl = ε·A_eff/d, with ε the electrolyte permittivity, A_eff the electrochemically accessible area, and d the double-layer thickness (Angstrom scale, fixed by ionic screening). At 1 kHz, for a typical metal electrode with geometric area A_geo = 100 μm², C_dl is on the order of 1–10 pF. Derivation: C_dl = ε_r·ε_0·A_eff/d. With ε_r ≈ 80 (water), ε_0 = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m, A_eff ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m², d ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m: C_dl ≈ 80·8.85×10⁻¹²·10⁻¹⁰/10⁻¹⁰ ≈ 7×10⁻¹² F = 7 pF. At 1 kHz, the capacitive reactance is 1/(2π·10³·7×10⁻¹²) ≈ 22.7 MΩ. This is the physical floor for a bare metal electrode of this geometry. Surface roughening increases A_eff, but only up to the limit where the pore depth exceeds the ionic diffusion length at the frequency of interest.

**❌ Engineering Misconception:** "Lower impedance at 1 kHz = solved charge injection." The 1 kHz impedance is a small-signal AC parameter. Neural stimulation and recording involve charge transfer across the double layer. The relevant metric for recording is the noise floor: thermal noise voltage V_n = sqrt(4k_B·T·R·Δf), where R is the real part of the interfacial impedance. For a 1 MΩ interface and 10 kHz bandwidth: V_n ≈ sqrt(4·1.38×10⁻²³·310·10⁶·10⁴) ≈ 4.1 μV_rms. Extracellular spike amplitudes are on the order of 50–500 μV_pp. The SNR is therefore bounded by the interface impedance, not by the amplifier. Reducing impedance from 1 MΩ to 100 kΩ improves SNR by sqrt(10) ≈ 3.2×, but only if the reduction is achieved without introducing Faradaic leakage that elevates the noise floor .
