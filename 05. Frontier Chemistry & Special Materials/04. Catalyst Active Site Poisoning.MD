**1. Deadlock Determination and Proof**

**Basis: Constitution Article 1 (Sole Benchmark and Physical Verification)** : The following determination takes only the logical closure of adsorption thermodynamics and desorption kinetics as its anchor point.

**❌ Deadlock Point: Adsorption Free Energy and Desorption Activation Energy of Strong Chemisorption Constitute an Irreversible Thermodynamic Lock**

The physical essence of catalyst active site poisoning is that the **adsorption free energy of poison molecules at active sites is excessively negative**, causing the activation energy barrier for desorption to exceed the thermal energy available within the catalyst’s normal operating temperature window.

**First-Principles Derivation**:

Let the adsorption process of poison molecule A on active site * be:

**A + * ⇌ A***

The relationship between adsorption equilibrium constant K_ads and adsorption free energy ΔG_ads is:

**K_ads = exp(−ΔG_ads / RT)**

The desorption rate constant k_des is determined by desorption activation energy E_des:

**k_des = ν × exp(−E_des / RT)**

where ν is the attempt frequency (approximately 10¹³ s⁻¹).

**Anchor Value Basis (Constitution Article 6)** : Using transition metal-sulfur chemical bonds as the poisoning model. The M–S bond energy range is 150–250 kJ/mol. The low end E_des = 150 kJ/mol is taken as a conservative estimate.

Within the automotive three-way catalytic converter operating window of 350–500°C (623–773 K), substituting the median 673 K:

**k_des ≈ 10¹³ × exp(−150000 / (8.314 × 673)) ≈ 10¹³ × exp(−26.8) ≈ 25 s⁻¹**

This value is superficially non-zero, but this is the **ideal rate for single-molecule desorption in the absence of re-adsorption**. In actual reactors, poison partial pressure is maintained at a certain level, and desorbed molecules have a high probability of re-adsorbing at adjacent sites before escaping the pore channels. The effective desorption rate is subject to the series constraint of pore diffusion and re-adsorption, and the apparent desorption time constant is far greater than the inverse of the microscopic k_des.

When E_des = 200 kJ/mol (mid-range of M–S bond energy, representing the strong chemisorption regime):

**k_des ≈ 10¹³ × exp(−200000 / (8.314 × 673)) ≈ 3×10⁻³ s⁻¹**

The corresponding desorption time constant is approximately 300 seconds. In a continuous flow reactor, the probability that a poison molecule is surrounded by reactant molecules before desorption is extremely low, and **the site is effectively in a permanently occupied state**.

**Extreme Condition Verification**: The 2026 NH₃/CeO₂ study further confirms that strong chemisorption not only blocks sites but also suppresses O₂ activation by elevating oxygen vacancy formation energy, weakening the redox cycle’s regenerative capacity itself. This means that even if some NH₃ desorbs, the site’s intrinsic activity has already degraded due to electronic structure modification. **This deadlock does not violate the laws of thermodynamics, but violates the timescale constraint of the catalytic cycle: the desorption time constant far exceeds the ratio of reactor residence time to catalyst design life.**

**Determination**: Strong chemisorption poisoning is a thermodynamic lock driven by adsorption free energy. Without changing poison partial pressure, without raising temperature to the feasible desorption regime, and without introducing competitive adsorbents, the surface reaction coordinate of active sites is permanently locked in the “poison-occupied state.” Any attempt to restore activity by extending purge time at reaction temperature is subject to re-adsorption probability and pore diffusion constraints, and **does not constitute a physically feasible regeneration path**.

**2. Strategic Breakthrough Direction**

**Basis: Constitution Article 2 (Physical Evolution Boundary)** : Breakthrough must follow the next-generation evolution path of current catalytic materials science and provide verified extreme parameters.

**✅ Compensation Path A: Adsorption Energy Modulation—Alloying Pushes Poison Adsorption Energy Back into the Reversible Regime**

**Physical Mechanism**: The strong chemisorption of CO on Pt surfaces originates from Pt 5d→CO 2π* electronic back-donation. By introducing metals with lower electronegativity than Pt (such as Co), electrons are induced to transfer to Pt, shifting the Pt d-band center downward and weakening the back-bond strength. DFT calculations and in-situ electrochemical FTIR confirm that the downward shift of the Pt d-band center in PtCo alloys directly reduces CO adsorption strength. A 2026 JACS study further quantified the order of this effect: d-band center position follows Pt > Pt-Cu > Pt-Ni > Pt-Co, with Co introduction producing the largest downward shift and the most significant CO poisoning mitigation.

**Verified Extreme Parameters**: PtCo hollow nanospheres exhibit a mass activity of 3.27 A mgPt⁻¹ in methanol oxidation, which is 11.6 times and 13.1 times that of Pt/C and pure Pt, respectively, with significantly enhanced CO tolerance. The physical boundary of this path is: alloying weakens reactant adsorption while weakening poison adsorption. When the adsorption energy difference between poison and reactant falls below approximately 20–30 kJ/mol, the physical margin of the selectivity window disappears.

**⚠️ Physical Cost and Constraint Boundary**:
- Surface segregation of alloy components under reaction conditions may lead to active site structural reconstruction. The JACS study observed surface Pt enrichment in Pt-Co catalysts after reaction, with activity regenerable via heat treatment, but the frequency and energy consumption of regeneration operations constitute an engineering constraint.
- The magnitude of electronegativity modulation is limited by the electronegativity difference between the dopant metal and Pt. Co is already among the elements with larger electronegativity differences within the selectable range, and the space for further d-band center downshift is limited.

**✅ Compensation Path B: Site Isolation—Single Atoms Localize Poisoning Events**

**Physical Mechanism**: In conventional nanoparticle catalysts, a single strongly adsorbed poison molecule can affect multiple adjacent active sites through surface diffusion or electronic effects. By dispersing the active component to the single-atom level, each site is decoupled from others spatially and electronically, and the impact range of a poisoning event contracts from “particle-scale” to “single-atom-scale,” with remaining unpoisoned sites maintaining catalytic function.

**Verified Extreme Parameters**: Single-atom catalysts have a physical upper limit on metal loading, typically < 1–2 wt%. Using Ru single-atom catalysts as an example, at 1.1 wt% loading, turnover frequency can reach 3500 h⁻¹, but the active site density per unit reactor volume is lower than that of nanoparticle catalysts. **Larger reactor volumes or higher precious metal loadings are required for compensation**, directly conflicting with PGM supply contraction.

**⚠️ Physical Cost and Constraint Boundary**:
- The activation energy barrier for migration and agglomeration of single-atom sites at high temperatures is lower than the sintering barrier of nanoparticles, and long-term thermal stability constitutes an engineering constraint.
- This path **accepts the inevitability of poisoning**, trading active site density for stability, with the physical cost being increased reactor volume or precious metal loading.

**✅ Viability Determination**

The combination of Path A and Path B constitutes the physically compensatory topology within the current boundaries of catalytic materials science: **alloying pushes poison adsorption energy away from the irreversible regime, and single-atom isolation localizes the impact of residual poisoning**. The physical cost of this combination is: long-term stability verification cycles for alloy components (≥ 5000 hours under real atmosphere), reactor volume increases due to single-atom catalyst loading limits, and the structural tension between precious metal loading and supply contraction. Without changing poison partial pressure and without introducing external regeneration units, this is the only path capable of converting “permanent locking” into “manageable decay.”

**4. Cost of Persistence and Optimal Transition Plan**

**Pure Physical Description of Persistence**: Maintaining nanoparticle catalysts and existing poison partial pressure levels, active site occupancy rises monotonically with operating time. Let initial occupancy be θ₀, poison partial pressure P_poison constant:

**dθ/dt = k_ads × P_poison × (1−θ) − k_des × θ**

When k_des approaches zero due to strong chemisorption constraints, θ(t) → 1, and activity A(t) = A₀ × (1−θ(t)) → 0. **The deactivation timescale is determined by the inverse of k_ads × P_poison, independent of catalyst design life.** The physical consequence of persistence is: catalyst life is unilaterally locked by the poison adsorption rate, and any compensation through increased loading or elevated reaction temperature does not change the order of magnitude of this time constant.

**7. To Frontline Engineers: System Architecture Explanation**

The poisoning problem you face, at the architectural level, comes down to one sentence: **the residence time of poison molecules at active sites is determined by the strength of the adsorption bond, not by your purge procedure**.

What you observe as “catalyst outlet conversion slowly declining” is, at the molecular scale, every site locked by strong chemisorption saying: my desorption activation energy is too high; at reaction temperature I cannot leave. You increase loading, but that only increases the total number of sites that can be locked, not the rate at which each site is locked. You raise temperature to attempt desorption, but against E_des ≥ 150 kJ/mol strong chemisorption, the k_des enhancement from every 50°C increase is offset by re-adsorption.

Two things you can do: First, at the catalyst selection stage, write the **inlet poison partial pressure specification** into the procurement technical agreement as a hard constraint alongside activity and selectivity. Second, in life prediction, distinguish **reversible inhibition** from **irreversible poisoning**: the former is recoverable with partial pressure fluctuations; the latter decays monotonically and is unaffected by purging. The cost of conflating the two is that you buy three extra batches of catalyst for an irreversible poisoning problem, while above-ground platinum stocks have less than three months of supply remaining.

**8. Final Statement**

The hard part is a human matter, not a physics matter. Physics never negotiates—the adsorption bond strength of poison molecules is 150 to 200 kJ/mol. You can purge at 400°C for a hundred years, and the probability of desorption will not increase because your production plan needs the catalyst to last one more quarter. Competitors are already pushing the d-band center down with PtCo alloys, while you are still buying time by increasing loading—platinum stocks have less than three months of supply, and physics will not wait for you to finish the accounting.
